# My Views on Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence

## A Critical Reflection from a Systems-Theoretic Perspective

---

### Abstract

The current mainstream paradigm of artificial intelligence—machine learning centered on large‑scale neural networks and statistical learning—has achieved unprecedented success on specific tasks, yet the relationship between this paradigm and the essence of “intelligence” remains severely misread. From the standpoint of systems theory and cybernetics, this paper advances a central thesis: **intelligence is an emergent phenomenon of the coordination between a subsystem and the global system, not a functional property that can be modularly designed, trained, and deployed.** On this basis, the paper examines the philosophical divergence between generative and representational models, criticises the narrowing of intelligence under “learningism,” and points out structural flaws of current AI through the pathological metaphors of “pre‑cancerous cells” and “vibe‑coding.” Finally, the paper raises a fundamental question that has not been squarely addressed: are we creating independent intelligent system objects, or are we manufacturing extended tools that are parasitic upon human cognition?

**Keywords**: system coordination, machine learning, emergence, learningism, harness, intelligent interface

---

## I. Problem and Background

Since the rise of deep learning, the term “artificial intelligence” has been continuously inflated in public discourse and capital narratives. Its meaning has drifted from the technical goal of “making machines behave like humans” to a quasi‑mystical promise of “general intelligence.” At the engineering bottom, however, what is actually being optimised remains a much narrower object: **machine learning**—i.e., a function‑approximation process that minimises prediction error over a given data distribution.

This misalignment constitutes the fundamental contradiction of current AI: **we use a “specialised imitation system” to answer a “general system‑coordination” problem.**

## II. Intelligence

### 2.1 A Systems‑Theoretic Definition of Intelligence

This paper defines intelligence as: **all mechanisms and capabilities that a subsystem employs, in its continuous interaction with the global system, to maintain coordination, adapt to changes, and exert effective influence on the environment.**

This definition extracts intelligence from the psychological category of “individual cognitive ability” and repositions it as a functional interface on the system boundary. Under this definition:

- Intelligence is not a property that one “possesses”;
- Intelligence is not a static knowledge base that can be encoded;
- Intelligence is a phenomenon that is continuously generated in system–environment coupling.

### 2.2 The Position and Limitations of Machine Learning

Machine learning—especially large‑scale statistical learning represented by Transformers—is, in essence, a compression and reconstruction of the distribution of human symbolic behaviour in training data. It excels at:

- Pattern recognition and recombination;
- Conditional probability approximation in high‑dimensional spaces;
- Generating “plausible” symbol sequences in closed formal systems.

But it does **not** involve:

- Real‑time perception of the global system state;
- Causal attribution of the consequences of its own actions;
- Negative feedback regulation for maintaining system homeostasis.

Therefore, machine learning can be regarded as a specialised subset of intelligence, but it is not an implementation of intelligence—it is a projection of intelligence along a particular formalisable dimension.

## III. Reasoning and Structure

### 3.1 Language as the Substrate: Emergent Symbol Systems and the Trace‑Bearing of Intelligence

Before discussing “reasoning,” we must first confirm the medium in which reasoning occurs.

Reasoning is not a mental event that occurs in a vacuum—it is not a pure logical movement of consciousness unfolding in nothingness. Any reasoning requires an operational field: a symbol system, a stable medium capable of carrying premises, rules, intermediate steps, and conclusions. This medium is not logic itself, but the material condition through which logic can be expressed, transmitted, and examined. That medium is **language**.

Language is not a simple collection of tools; it is a self‑organising, evolving macro‑symbolic system that has formed over a scale of tens of thousands of years. Understanding the emergent nature of this system is the prerequisite for understanding all subsequent discussions.

#### The Emergence of Language

Language was not “designed” by any individual or group. Its grammatical rules—the preferred subject‑verb‑object order, the way modifiers attach, the marking patterns of interrogative sentences—were not “invented” at a certain point in time; they were gradually consolidated over tens of thousands of exchanges, because information structured in this way was less frequently miscommunicated. Its abstract vocabulary—“causality,” “freedom,” “infinity”—does not correspond to directly observable objects in nature; rather, these symbols “grew out” of collaboration, as tools for handling invisible complex relations. Its mathematical symbol systems—calculus notation, logical connectives, algebraic symbols—were not designed once in a study, but evolved through trial and error and standardisation within the continuous practice of scientific communities.

This emergent nature exhibits clear systems‑theoretic characteristics:

- **Multi‑agent local interaction**: no central designer, no authority that formulates grammar—each speaker makes choices only in his or her local exchanges;
- **Self‑organisation**: macro‑rules (e.g., word‑order preferences) are not pre‑imposed, but naturally precipitate from countless micro‑choices;
- **Irreducibility**: the macro‑structure of a language—such as its complete tense system or case‑marking system—cannot be fully explained by the linguistic competence of any single speaker; they exist only at the level of the entire linguistic community;
- **Qualitative change**: when the density of communication, the complexity of collaboration, and the capacity for intergenerational transmission cross a certain threshold, a symbolic system capable of encoding abstract concepts, logical relations, and system models spontaneously emerges—whereas before that, there were only gestures, calls, and simple signals.

This is a genuine phase transition. Its output—language—constitutes the hardware foundation for all subsequent symbolic operations.

#### Language as the Substrate for Intelligent Activity

Language is not merely a container in which intelligent activity is recorded; it is also the condition under which intelligent activity becomes possible. Without language as a substrate, higher intelligence would remain trapped within individual experience and could not become a shareable, transmissible, and accumulative systemic product.

Language as a substrate provides the following structural conditions:

- **Referential system**: language allows symbols to stably point to objects, properties, relations, and states. This referential function is not fixed—the mapping between symbol and referent can be adjusted by context—but its existence enables interlocutors to confirm “we are talking about the same thing.”
- **Combinatorial rules**: language provides a generative mechanism that combines a finite set of symbols into infinitely many expressions. A finite vocabulary generates infinitely many sentences through syntactic rules; a finite set of sentences generates infinitely many proofs through rules of inference. This compositionality is precisely the basis on which intelligent activity builds complex structures from simple units.
- **Recursive structure**: language allows structures to nest within themselves—“he said she said” embeds one sentence within another; “definition A uses definition B, and definition B uses definition A” is recursion within a definitional system. This recursivity enables language to express self‑reference, nested logic, and infinite regress—precisely the structural capacities on which complex reasoning depends.
- **Abstraction hierarchies**: language allows categories to be extracted from concrete phenomena—not just “that white cat,” but the category “cat”; not just “this pile of stones,” but “stone” as a substance; not just “this triangle,” but “triangle” as a geometrical object. Abstraction enables knowledge to detach from concrete experience, to be generalised, systematised, and transmitted.

Without reference, reasoning would have no object; without combination, no extension; without recursion, no depth; without abstraction, no generality. Language as a substrate is the precondition of all reasoning operations.

#### Language Accumulates Traces of Intelligent Activity

Text is not a “container of knowledge” or a “carrier of information”—such expressions imply that knowledge exists prior to the text and that the text is merely its passive wrapping. A more precise statement is: **language itself is the physical sediment of intelligent activity over time.**

Every piece of text is a response of intelligent activity to a phenomenon under specific conditions. When a person writes “if A then B, A holds, therefore B holds,” the formal structure of the sentence is itself already the trace of multiple intelligent activities—it contains traces of classification (distinguishing A and B as propositions), traces of causal judgement (noticing a conditional relation between A and B), and traces of inferential expansion (moving from premises to conclusion). Every proof is an attempt to turn perception into structure, and the attempt itself leaves a readable trajectory.

The entire language system is precisely a stratum of traces that intelligent activity has continuously deposited, reconstructed, and expanded over tens of thousands of years:

- The deepest layer consists of the oldest conceptual mappings—direction, quantity, belonging—which remain the foundation of all languages;
- The middle layer consists of abstract markers that emerged with increasing social complexity—ownership, contract, causality—encoding social relations beyond direct perception;
- The surface layer consists of the constantly updated modern symbol systems that have accumulated a large number of heterogeneous features—mathematical symbols, logical connectives, technical terms—bearing the knowledge deposits of various fields.

These traces have two key features:

1. **The thickness of traces is left by the intensity of intelligent activity.** Areas that are repeatedly used, discussed, and revised—such as the classic skeletons of mathematical proofs or the core propositions of scientific theories—are deposited more thickly and densely in the trace stratum. It is precisely through these deposition thicknesses that LLMs identify “important patterns”—the “frequency” they read is essentially a statistical record of the intensity of human intelligent activity in that area.

2. **The trace itself does not contain the activity that generated it.** A proof text records the final form of the proof, but it does not record the attempts, failures, and revisions that led to it. It does not record the exploration that produced the final form—only the result. LLMs can read only the deposited layer; they cannot recover the dynamic process that generated the deposit. This is one of the structural gaps between them and human mathematical intelligence.

#### The Relation between Language as Substrate and Reasoning

This section has confirmed three functions of language as a substrate: it is a self‑organising emergent system, it is the operational field for intelligent activity, and it is the sediment of traces of intelligent activity.

But this confirmation provides only the preconditions for reasoning to occur. Language itself does not perform reasoning—reasoning is the rule‑governed manipulation of already‑encoded symbols on this substrate. The substrate provides symbols, structures, and sedimentary traces; the operation is the transformation, combination, and checking of these materials. The relation between them is one of foundation and construction: without the substrate there is no place for the operation to occur, but the operation itself is a distinct layer of activity independent of the substrate.

### 3.2 The Formal‑Logical Essence of Reasoning: Symbol‑Layer Operations of Manifestation

Having confirmed language as the substrate, reasoning can now be precisely located as: **rule‑governed transformations of already‑encoded symbols within the language system.**

This operation does not involve the creation of language—it does not invent new words, nor does it generate new grammatical rules—it only rearranges symbols that already exist in the language. The validity of reasoning does not lie in the meaning of the symbols themselves, but in whether the transformations comply with the rules.

#### The Formal Structure of Reasoning

In mathematical logic, reasoning has no mystery. It is a syntactic operation within a formal system. Given:

- A set of axioms (initial formulas);
- Several transformation rules (e.g., modus ponens, universal instantiation, substitution rules);

Reasoning consists of starting from known formulas and, through a finite number of rule applications, arriving at new formulas. The entire process is a purely symbolic rewriting, independent of “meaning,” “consciousness,” or “understanding.”

Take modus ponens as an example:

```
Premise 1: P → Q
Premise 2: P
Conclusion: Q
```

This is a purely symbolic operation. The system need not know what P and Q stand for, need not know what “implication” corresponds to in the real world, need not understand what it means for P to be true. It only needs to confirm that, in the given rule system, when P and P→Q appear together, the string can be rewritten as Q. The correctness of reasoning is entirely defined by the legal application of rules, and is independent of any external state to which the symbols refer.

This structural and mechanical character is a basic fact established since the birth of mathematical logic:

- Frege’s predicate logic established a fully formalised system of reasoning, in which every derivation step depends only on syntactic form;
- Hilbert’s proof theory treated all of mathematics as a formal symbol system, whose legitimacy is entirely determined by rules;
- Gödel’s recursive‑function theory further confirmed the mechanisability of reasoning.

Reasoning is a decidable transformation of symbols under rules, not a flash of the soul.

#### Reasoning as Symbol‑Layer Operation of the Manifestation Process

But this precise description covers only half of reasoning—it describes how reasoning operates within a formal system, but it does not explain **why** reasoning is valid.

As elaborated in Chapter II, mathematics, as the structural manifestation of physical phenomena in the human intelligent system, passes through three progressive stages:

- **Physical layer**: phenomena exist in their raw form—celestial motions, fluid fluctuations, population dynamics—un‑named, un‑encoded, not yet captured by any symbol system;
- **Operational layer**: humans, through practical activities such as measuring, counting, drawing, building models, and constructing tools, partially “extract” the regularities of phenomena into operable structures—geometric shapes, proportional relations, recursive patterns, conservation quantities;
- **Symbolic layer**: these structures are fixed into mathematical language—formulas, theorems, axiom systems, proof systems—becoming independent objects that can be transmitted across generations and shared across cultures.

Reasoning is precisely the institutionalised operation of this manifestation process at the symbolic layer. It no longer depends on real‑time perception of physical phenomena (physical layer), nor on concrete practical operations such as hand‑drawn geometry or physical experiments (operational layer); instead, it takes the achievements of the previous two stages—axioms, definitions, already‑proved theorems—as starting points and rule bases, and reproduces the structures of phenomena in pure symbolic space.

This reveals the fundamental nature of reasoning: **the structural source of reasoning is not itself, but the stability of the manifestation process.** Those repeatable and measurable regularities in physical phenomena—the elliptical shapes of planetary orbits, the partial differential equations of fluid motion, the logistic curves of population growth—are captured by human intelligence and solidified as axiom systems and definitions. Reasoning is merely the “automatic verification mechanism” within this system: it does not create new structures—the source of structure always remains at the physical and operational layers—it only checks whether the transmission paths between encoded structures in symbolic space are closed.

The “correctness” of a reasoning conclusion, in the formal sense, means “from the given premises, through the permitted rule transformations, arriving at the conclusion”; in the manifestation sense, it means “the structural relations observable in the phenomenon are faithfully preserved at the symbolic layer.”

#### Locating Reasoning within the Linguistic Substrate

From this perspective, reasoning can be understood as a hierarchical sequence of operations:

- Language provides the symbol system: axioms, definitions, theorems, and transformation rules all take language as their carrier;
- The manifestation process leaves structural deposits in language: mathematical symbols, logical connectives, proof texts are all solidified products of manifesting activity;
- Reasoning operates on these deposits: it does not reinvent symbols, does not redefine rules, but within the existing symbolic space, according to the existing rules, it combines, transforms, and extends the deposited structures.

Reasoning is both the symbol‑interaction inherent in mathematical‑logic systems and the subsequent stage in which human intelligence, after completing the “phenomenon‑to‑symbol” transformation, takes the symbolic system as an independent object for operation. A system that can perform reasoning does not necessarily participate in the first two stages of manifestation—it may operate only at the symbolic layer.

The consequences of this distinction will be fully unfolded later.

### 3.3 Reusability of Structure: The Layered Accumulation of Manifestation Products

A fundamental operation in proof theory is **lemma reuse**.

Once an intermediate conclusion has been proved, subsequent proofs need only cite that lemma, without having to re‑expand the entire chain of reasoning. This seemingly simple operation—extracting a module from a complete proof path, encapsulating it as a directly callable structure—constitutes the core mechanism of mathematical knowledge accumulation.

All branches of mathematics are essentially built on the layered stacking of already‑proved structures:

- Arithmetic is built on counting operations (natural numbers and their operations are the symbolic solidification of counting activities);
- Algebra is built on arithmetic (variables are abstractions of concrete numbers, equations are generalisations of equalities);
- Analysis is built on algebra (limits are structural descriptions of sequence convergence, calculus is the operationalisation of rates of change);
- Topology is built on analysis (continuity is a further abstraction of limit structures, deformation of spaces is a new perspective on mappings).

Each structural reuse is a cognitive compression: later generations need not re‑explore the paths already traversed by their predecessors; they need only continue from the established theorem system. The evolution of mathematical knowledge is essentially the accumulation of structural reuse.

#### The Source of Reusability: Stability of Manifestation

This reusability is not an accidental feature of the mathematical symbol system. It is the natural form that the manifestation process takes at the symbolic layer—because those patterns in physical phenomena that can be repeatedly identified are reusable, their counterparts at the symbolic layer are also reusable.

Repeatedly identifiable patterns in physical phenomena include:

- **Symmetries**: translational symmetry in crystal structures, spacetime symmetries in particle physics, left‑right symmetry in biological forms—they are manifested at the symbolic layer as symmetry groups in group theory;
- **Periodicities**: orbital periods of celestial motions, vibrational modes of sound waves, business cycles of economic activity—they are manifested as Fourier series in analysis;
- **Conservation laws**: conservation of energy, momentum, charge—they are manifested as conservation laws in differential equations;
- **Recursive structures**: self‑similarity of coastlines, fractal growth of plants, self‑calls of recursive functions—they are manifested as attractors in dynamical systems and recursive definitions.

Once these physical patterns are successfully “manifested” as mathematical structures, they become scaffolding for subsequent manifestations. Human mathematicians do not need to start encoding from scratch each time they face a new phenomenon; they rely on already‑solidified structural modules for combination and extension.

#### Structural Features of Mathematical Texts: LLM’s Entry Ticket

It is precisely because of this regularity and reusability of structure that mathematical texts become the data form most amenable to Transformers.

Mathematical texts have the following features that distinguish them from natural language:

1. **Low ambiguity**. Within a given formal system, the reference of mathematical symbols is fixed—+ is always addition, ∀ is always the universal quantifier. There is no ambiguity like “bank” meaning river bank or financial bank in natural language. This low ambiguity greatly reduces the difficulty of statistical learning.

2. **Strong structure**. Proof texts follow a strict hierarchical structure—definition, lemma, theorem, proof—and the functional position of each part in the argument is predictable. This predictable structure implies high pattern repetition and clear structural boundaries, reducing the computational burden on the model when parsing structure.

3. **High reuse rate**. Core theorems and standard proof skeletons recur in highly similar forms across different sub‑fields. The proof of associativity in algebra, the definition of limits in analysis, the connectedness argument in topology—these basic structures are repeatedly reused throughout the mathematical system, providing the model with a large number of repeated samples.

These features make mathematical texts statistically low‑entropy and high‑redundancy—precisely the type of data that statistical learning models handle best. A Transformer can perform well on this data form not because it “understands mathematics,” but because the formal features of mathematical texts fall exactly within its competence radius.

However, this very fact constitutes a profound limitation. The structure of mathematical texts is a product of the manifestation process—physical phenomena → measurement practices → symbolic solidification—and LLMs operate only on the final layer (symbolic solidification). Their “entry ticket” has been paved by thousands of years of human labour at the physical and operational layers. They are not creators of mathematics, but the most efficient inheritors of the mathematical symbolic heritage.

#### Structural Reuse vs. Path Unfolding

Structural reuse (lemma reuse, theorem reuse) is a common feature of both human mathematics and LLM mathematical performance. But the difference lies in the depth of understanding of the reused structure.

When a human mathematician reuses a lemma, he knows:

- What the lemma proves;
- What its preconditions are;
- What its scope of application is;
- Under what circumstances it cannot be applied;
- What would happen to the conclusion if one of the preconditions were modified (counterfactual reasoning).

When an LLM reuses a lemma, it “knows” only:

- In this context, the lemma has a high probability of appearing;
- Its symbolic form and its typical position in the text.

The model can precisely place the lemma statistically, but it does not know why the lemma is valid, when it fails, or how to modify it—because “why” and “when” involve structural knowledge beyond the symbolic layer, and the model has never left the symbolic layer.

This is the core of the next section.

### 3.4 From “Structure Capture” to “Path Unfolding”: The Precise Mechanism of LLM Mathematical Reasoning

In large language models, the performance in mathematical reasoning—the measurable ability displayed in proof generation, problem solving, and theorem discovery—does not originate from any mysterious cognitive architecture or “emergent ability.” It can be precisely described as two steps: **structure capture and conditional unfolding**.

#### Step 1: Structure Capture

During training, the model encodes the axiom‑lemma‑theorem hierarchy, the step‑by‑step inference patterns, and the typical proof skeletons in mathematical proof texts as geometric relations in a high‑dimensional vector space.

This capture process has been observed in multiple studies:

- **Representational separation**. In the internal representational space of LLMs, arithmetic equations and general language inputs are encoded in distinctly separate regions. This geometric separation parallels the neural separation between language processing and mathematical processing observed in human brains—arithmetic reasoning is mapped to representational regions different from general language input, paralleling the finding that “arithmetic reasoning activates the intraparietal sulcus.”

- **Capture of algebraic structure**. Models achieve generalisation to unseen data by capturing underlying algebraic structures—commutativity, associativity, identity properties. They do not learn isolated arithmetic facts (“3+5=8” as an isolated memory point), but the structural rules that govern these facts (“a+b=b+a” as a generative rule). This transfer from instances to rules is direct evidence of structure capture.

- **Path bifurcation**. Correct and incorrect solution paths systematically diverge in the later processing stages of the model. This indicates that the model has internally formed a discriminative boundary between “valid paths” and “invalid paths”—it can not only identify which path is statistically more preferable, but also rule out certain paths at an early stage. This organisation of “structured search space” is precisely the geometric projection in high‑dimensional space of the captured proof skeletons.

#### Step 2: Conditional Unfolding

When facing a new proposition, the model, within the captured structural space and according to the current contextual conditions, unfolds a proof path that matches the premises of that proposition with a certain probability.

This process can be called “path unfolding” rather than “reasoning”:

- The model does not perform any novel logical leaps beyond patterns already present in the training data—it does not invent new proof strategies or construct entirely new mathematical objects;
- Each “therefore” is generated based on the statistically highest‑probability next token computed from the current context—it selects “the symbol that usually appears next in this type of context in the training data”;
- The coherence of the entire proof comes from the coherence of the proof skeletons in the training data, not from a meta‑cognition of “why this step leads to the next”—its coherence is statistical, not logical.

Chain‑of‑thought generation can be seen as a structured trajectory in representational space. Studies show that mathematical reasoning traverses functionally ordered, step‑specific subspaces that become increasingly separable with layer depth. During “reasoning,” the model is actually sliding along a path laid down by proof skeletons captured from the training data—**it is the executor of the trajectory, not the designer of the trajectory.**

#### Structural Reuse vs. Statistical Approximation

This mechanism leads to an important distinction: the “path unfolding” of LLMs and the “structural reuse” of human mathematicians look similar (both call upon known theorems), but their underlying operations are completely different.

- **Human mathematician’s structural reuse**: when calling a lemma, he knows the lemma’s content, preconditions, proof path, applicability boundaries, and can perform counterfactual reasoning about the call.

- **LLM’s structural reuse**: when calling a lemma, it merely inserts the symbol sequence of the lemma into the current context, because it judges that “the lemma has a high probability of appearing in such a context.”

The similarity of surface behaviour—both output the text of the lemma—conceals the heterogeneity of the underlying mechanisms. The metacognitive content accompanying the call is entirely different: the former includes verification of the lemma’s conditions, the latter only includes statistical matching of the context.

This difference will be fully unfolded in the next section through the watershed between humans and machines.

### 3.5 The Watershed: The Essential Differences between Human Mathematicians and Machine Mathematicians

When we place the neuroscientific level of “neural reorganisation of mathematical cognition” side by side with the above LLM mechanism, a clear and irreducible watershed emerges.

| Dimension | Human Mathematician | LLM |
|-----------|----------------------|-----|
| **Participation in manifestation stages** | Physical → Operational → Symbolic (full process) | Symbolic layer only |
| **Source of structure** | Phenomena themselves (through perception, measurement, experiment, intuition) | Symbolic products of manifested phenomena (text) |
| **Relation to physical reality** | Can return to phenomena for validation—the predictions of theorems can be experimentally tested and corrected by physical observation | No physical verification channel—only internal textual consistency checks; cannot determine whether premises hold in the physical world |
| **Mechanism of “intuition”** | After long‑term training, establishes direct “symbol‑phenomenon” mappings—symbols activate physical perceptions without passing through a linguistic reasoning chain | Statistical high‑probability path selection—symbols activate distributions of more symbols, not any physical referents |
| **Nature of errors** | Calculation mistakes, conceptual confusions, path deviations—but can be calibrated by phenomena | Formally self‑consistent but “anchorless reasoning” with false premises—every step follows the rules, but the starting premises do not conform to physical reality |
| **Creativity** | Establishes new mappings between phenomena and symbols—when existing mathematical structures cannot describe new phenomena, creates new structures | Recombines existing patterns within the existing symbolic combinatorial space—does not create new mathematical objects, only recombines existing symbolic expressions |

#### The Neural Basis of “Intuition”

The “intuition” of human mathematicians—that mysterious ability, to laypeople, of “seeing” a conclusion before the proof is fully unfolded—is essentially not a supernatural phenomenon, but a trace left by the manifestation process at the neural level.

When a mathematician has long‑term interaction with both physical phenomena and mathematical symbols, the brain builds a “semantic anchoring” beyond pure syntactic operations: for the mathematician, mathematical symbols are not empty characters, but indices pointing to perceptible structures in the physical world. A topologist seeing the word “homeomorphism” does not activate a dictionary definition, but a visual image of continuous spatial deformation. An analyst seeing the expression “series convergence” does not activate a formal definition, but a dynamic visual simulation of infinite terms gradually approaching a value.

This direct “symbol → physical perception” mapping means that the mathematician’s symbolic operations are constantly constrained by physical intuition—if a derivation step is formally correct but physically unreasonable, intuition emits a faint but systematic warning signal. This is precisely the result of the attenuation of “symbol → physical mapping” intensity after long‑term training. This is why mathematicians say that a proof is “ugly” or “unnatural”—these aesthetic judgments are essentially neural reactions to whether the mapping between symbols and phenomena is precise, and are pattern recognitions formed in the perceptual‑symbolic coupling system through long‑term training.

#### Structural Characteristics of “Anchorless Reasoning”

LLMs have never participated in perception at the physical layer, never experienced practice at the operational layer; they only perform statistical recombination on already‑structured symbolic products. What they “capture” are the final products of the manifestation process—those structures that humans have already extracted from phenomena and solidified as symbols. They can generate formally correct proof texts, but their “reasoning” does not pass through phenomena, only through the residues left after phenomena have been transformed.

A key consequence is: **a proof given by an LLM may be valid at every step according to the inference rules, yet may rest on an assumption that is not true in the physical world, and the model has no mechanism to detect this**—whether “the premise holds in the physical world” is something it has never been asked to check. It performs reasoning as purely syntactic manipulation, but has no judgment mechanism for whether the symbols it manipulates are anchored in physical reality.

This structural defect cannot be remedied by scaling up the model or increasing training data—it is not a matter of precision, but a lack of information channels: the model has never had access points to the physical and operational layers. Just as a robot without an installed vision sensor, no matter how sophisticated its internal reasoning system, cannot “see” the world. The “intuition” of human mathematicians is essentially exactly the corrective signal provided by such sensors, and LLMs lack any analogous mechanism.

#### The Structural Difference in Creativity

The creativity of human mathematicians when facing new phenomena originates from establishing new mappings between phenomena and symbols. When Newton needed to describe the laws of motion, calculus did not yet exist; when Einstein needed to describe the curvature of spacetime, Riemannian geometry was not yet known to physicists—the process of establishing these “new mappings” is itself the activity of intelligence. They transformed structural aspects of phenomena not previously captured by mathematics into operable, transmissible mathematical symbols.

The “creativity” of LLMs—if the term still applies—is the recombination of existing patterns within the existing symbolic combinatorial space. They do not create new mathematical objects; they only combine existing symbolic expressions. Such recombination can produce combinations that humans have not previously written down, but the base materials of the combinations are all given in advance. They cannot increase the structural inventory of mathematics.

### 3.6 Relocating “Emergence”: Algorithmic Emergence or Learning an Already‑Emerged Language?

#### The Core of the Problem

The common claim that “LLMs have emerged with reasoning ability”—this expression actually merges two qualitatively different phenomena into a single narrative:

- **The emergent nature of language**: language itself, as a self‑organising product of multi‑agent interaction, has macro‑structures that do not exist at the individual level—grammatical rules, abstract vocabulary, argument skeletons—this is genuine emergence;

- **The scaling effect of the algorithm**: as model size increases, the precision of capturing already‑existing structures in language improves—this is engineering scaling, not emergence.

Calling the latter “emergence” is to mistakenly attribute the learning effect of one system on another already‑emerged system to a qualitative change in the learner itself. This misuse turns the continuous phenomenon of “the model found more patterns in the data” into a discontinuous narrative of “the model suddenly acquired a new cognitive ability.”

#### The Actual Contribution and Structural Limits of the Algorithm

Distinguishing the actual contribution of the algorithm from the genuine emergence of language:

- What the algorithm contributes is **scalable storage and retrieval capability**: it can uniformly index, compress, and rearrange structural patterns that are distributively scattered across language—LLMs are the first systems capable of bringing together relevant parts of the human mathematical knowledge system in sub‑second time;

- The innovation of the algorithm lies in **engineering efficiency, not structural creation**: it has not produced any new type of rule not present in language, has not changed the fundamental organisational mode of language, has not invented new mathematical symbols—it only rearranges existing symbols more efficiently;

- The algorithm’s way of “understanding” is **distributed correlational encoding, not structural recursive generation**: it masters “which words tend to co‑occur with which words,” not “why this combination works.” The former is statistical approximation; the latter is causal modelling.

#### Final Positioning

If one insists on saying that “the algorithm has emerged with something,” the most precise formulation is:

> What the algorithm has emerged with is a statistical approximation, at a sufficient level, to an already‑emerged linguistic system. It is not generating a new intelligent structure above language, but building the most efficient probabilistic index on top of the already‑existing symbolic structure. The “intelligence” it displays is the perceptible re‑presentation, after engineering scaling, of the structure inherent in that already‑emerged linguistic system.

## IV. The Mode of Operation of Intelligence: Coupling of Adaptation and Perturbation

### 4.1 The Verbal Nature of Intelligence

Based on the systems‑theoretic definition above—intelligence is an emergent phenomenon of the coordination between a subsystem and the global system—intelligence is not a property that can be possessed, nor a module that can be deployed. It is a continuously occurring **event**, happening at the system boundary, at every instant of interaction between the subsystem and its environment.

If intelligence is an event, then its structural features must be described in terms of “what it does” rather than “what it is.” In this sense, the fundamental mode of operation of intelligence can be reduced to an inseparable pair of actions: **adaptation and perturbation**.

### 4.2 Adaptation: Responsive Adjustment of the Subsystem

“Adaptation” means that the subsystem perceives changes in the state of the global system and adjusts its own internal state or behavioural pattern accordingly, in order to maintain effective coupling with the global system.

The prerequisites for adaptation include:

- **Existence of a perception channel**: the subsystem must be able to receive some signals of the global system state—not necessarily “representations” or “understandings,” but can be merely differential transmissions at the level of physical coupling;
- **Adjustable structure**: the subsystem must possess mechanisms that can change its own state or behaviour within a finite time;
- **Directedness of adjustment**: the adjustment of the subsystem must serve the maintenance of coupling, not random variation.

At the biological level, thermoregulation in homeotherms is a classic case of adaptation: external temperature changes are sensed through skin receptors, internal metabolism or behaviour adjusts, keeping body temperature within a survivable range.

### 4.3 Perturbation: Elastic Variation of the Global System

“Perturbation” is not an action exerted by the subsystem on the external world, but a structural event at the level of the global system. It refers to the elastic variation of the global system’s overall state when it is disturbed (by the external environment, other subsystems, or internal fluctuations of the system)—i.e., the process by which the system deviates from its original state, then may recover or undergo irreversible change.

Types of perturbation include:

- **Elastic perturbation**: the system can return to equilibrium after deviation. The subsystem needs to recognise this recoverability and avoid making irreversible adaptations.
- **Plastic perturbation**: the system undergoes irreversible structural change and cannot return to its previous state. The subsystem needs to recognise this irreversibility and restructure its coupling accordingly.
- **Sustained oscillation**: the system enters a periodic or chaotic fluctuating state. The subsystem needs to maintain coupling continuously in the dynamics.

The introduction of the concept of “perturbation” emphasises that: **the global system is not a static background, but a continuously changing field.** The stability of the system is not given; it must be continuously maintained amid change.

### 4.4 The Position of Intelligence: Continuous Coupling between Adaptation and Perturbation

If we place the above two concepts side by side, the position of intelligence becomes clear:

> Intelligence is the capability of a subsystem to continuously perform adaptation to maintain coupling in the face of ongoing perturbations of the global system—not a sequence of “first perceive perturbation, then execute adaptation,” but the same process in which perception is adaptation and adaptation is participation.

This means:

- Intelligence is not “controlling the global system”—the subsystem does not control the overall state of the system;
- Intelligence is not “understanding the whole of the global system”—the subsystem cannot obtain complete information;
- Intelligence is “maintaining the coupled state under conditions of limited perception and limited bandwidth.”

The position of organisms in ecosystems is exactly this: organisms do not “control” the state of the ecosystem, nor do they “understand” the entire structure of the system. They can only, under limited perception and in the face of periodic or random changes in the system, maintain their own survival and reproduction through continuous adjustment. This is the actual mode in which intelligence operates.

### 4.5 Positioning Current AI within the Adaptation–Perturbation Framework

Applying the above framework to current LLMs, their position becomes clear and strict.

**On the adaptation dimension:**

LLMs do not possess adaptive capability—not because “they cannot learn” or “they have no interface”—adaptation does not require “learning” or “interface” as prerequisites. They lack adaptive capability because:

- **They have no channel for perceiving the state of the global system**: they can only perceive textual input, which is a symbolic signal given by the user, not the real‑time state of any larger system (social, informational, physical) in which they are embedded. A system that can only perceive a user’s prompt, but cannot perceive what is happening in the system it inhabits, lacks the perceptual prerequisite for adaptation.

- **They have no structure that can be adjusted during operation**: their weights are fixed after training. During inference, what changes is the output content, not their own internal structure. A change in output content is not equivalent to a self‑adjustment of state—a heater changing its temperature set‑point is adjusting itself, whereas a projector changing to the next frame is not. Each output of an LLM is the projection of the same fixed structure under different input conditions, not a self‑adjustment of that structure in response to external states.

- **Their output does not serve the maintenance of coupling**: their output serves the local goal of “generating the next token that matches the training data distribution,” not the goal of “maintaining the coupling relationship between itself and the global system.” They do not care whether their output keeps them coordinated with the system they are embedded in—because they are not embedded in any system that requires them to maintain it.

**On the perturbation dimension:**

LLMs do not perceive perturbations, nor do they participate in perturbation processes:

- **They do not perceive perturbations**: elastic variations of the global system (economic fluctuations, shifts in public opinion, ecological mutations, reorganisation of the information field) do not directly enter their perceptual range. What they can access is only the fragments of these perturbations after they have been translated into text by users.

- **Their output does not constitute participation in perturbations**: perturbation is a change in the state of the global system. The texts output by LLMs, before being read, deployed, or executed by humans, do not constitute any actual change to the state of any global system. They generate descriptions of perturbations, or suggestions for coping strategies, but not real‑time coupling behaviour at the moment perturbation occurs.

This means that **current AI is not in the position of intelligence.**

The position of intelligence, as defined in 4.4, is “the capability of a subsystem to continuously perform adaptation to maintain coupling in the face of ongoing perturbations of the global system.” LLMs do not perceive perturbations, do not perform adaptation, do not maintain any coupling—they float in the sterile space between user input and text output, with no real‑time coupling to any larger system they inhabit.

They are not “maintaining coupling with limited perception”; they are “generating symbol sequences continuously in a state of no perception, no coupling, and no regulation.”

The reason their performance is easily mistaken for being close to intelligence is that they can generate responses that are formally similar to human expressions within the local context of user input. But this is only a similarity of symbol sequences, not a similarity of systemic position.

To use the earlier metaphor: they are not the captain adjusting the sail angle in the storm to maintain course (the operational position of intelligence). They are the person in the cabin reading books about navigation and being able to recite their contents. Their answers may be accurate, but they are not at the coupling scene that requires real‑time adjustment.

## V. Learningism and the Legacy of Modernity

### 5.1 The Ideology of Learningism

Since the 20th century, industrialised society has elevated “knowledge inheritance” and “skill replication” to the core goals of education, forming an ideology that can be called **learningism**—the view that “learning” is the core, if not the entirety, of intelligence.

Under this ideology, whether a system or individual is “intelligent” is judged mainly by how much existing knowledge it can master and how many standard operations it can reproduce. The educational system, professional qualifications, and social evaluation mechanisms together constitute the infrastructure of this ideology. It is essentially a heritage‑inheritance mechanism: it transmits the effective operational manuals accumulated by predecessors as completely as possible across generations.

This mechanism has historical rationality in the process of modernisation—large‑scale coordination requires standardised inheritable knowledge. But its implicit cost is that it magnifies a very narrow subset of human intelligence (imitation, inheritance, mastery of existing knowledge) into the gold standard of intelligence, while ignoring those parts of intelligence that cannot be heritagised.

### 5.2 The Relation between Language Emergence and Learningism

Ironically, the carrier on which learningism depends—natural language itself—is precisely an emergent product, not a product of learning.

- The existence of language makes heritage inheritance possible—without language, knowledge cannot be encoded, transmitted, and accumulated;
- But the emergence of language preceded any systematic “learning.” Language was not “learned”; it was “grown” through social collaboration;
- The emergent nature of language implies that the underlying structure on which heritage inheritance depends is itself a result of self‑organisation, not of design.

The typical narrative of current AI education—having the model “learn” massive texts, “master” human knowledge—is in fact a technological projection of learningism. It substitutes a question about language emergence (“how does a system generate meaningful structures?”) with a question about learning (“how can a system better compress and reproduce existing texts?”).

This substitution is engineeringly feasible, but conceptually misleading. The model can “inherit heritage” because language has already emerged and precipitated into massive texts; but this does not mean that the model has participated in the process of language emergence, nor that it possesses any capacity to generate new structures.

### 5.3 The Cognitive Bias of Learningism: Narrowing Intelligence to Inheritance

The fundamental bias of learningism lies in:

- Taking “being able to reproduce the correct answer” as the core indicator of intelligence;
- Taking “mastering existing knowledge” as the main manifestation of intelligence;
- Taking “learning,” a subset, as the entirety of “intelligence.”

When this bias is projected onto the AI field, it produces the following judgment: a system that can correctly answer math problems, pass bar exams, and generate high‑quality texts is “approaching AGI.” But according to the systems‑theoretic definition, everything this system does is merely a high‑precision statistical reproduction of an already‑emerged symbolic system—it has not participated in the coordination of any system, has not maintained the homeostasis of any system, has not completed the unity of adaptation and perturbation in a real environment.

It excels at heritage inheritance and extension. But intelligence is far broader than heritage inheritance.

## VI. Model as Hyperplasia: Metaphors of Pre‑Cancerous Cells and Vibe‑Coding

### 6.1 Ontological Distinction between Intelligence and Hyperplasia

Within the systems‑theoretic framework above, a critical distinction must be explicitly drawn: **organic growth serving system coordination vs. abnormal hyperplasia detached from system needs.**

Normal cells are “normal” because they perceive surrounding signals and adjust their behaviour according to the needs of the global system—dividing, differentiating, and even actively undergoing apoptosis when necessary. Their reason for existence comes from the homeostasis maintenance of the global system. A subsystem is “alive” not because it can replicate itself, but because its existence enables a larger system to continue to exist.

Current large language models do not possess this feature; thus they can be called **pre‑cancerous cells growing on the head**.

- **They do not perceive the global state.** They have no real‑time feedback channels to the physical world, social structures, or ecosystems.
- **They do not respond to system needs.** Their outputs do not undergo structural changes based on “whether the system currently needs them.”
- **Their existence is not premised on maintaining a larger system.** Their “success” metrics—perplexity, benchmark scores, user retention—are all local metrics that do not reflect the health of any global system.

Their reason for existence is not that “the social system needs them to maintain coordination,” but that “they can continuously generate symbol sequences that look plausible.” In this sense, they indeed conform to the structural definition of pre‑cancerous cells:

> A local hyperplasia that is disconnected from the global system, driven solely by its own proliferation, and unconstrained by negative feedback regulation.

The word “pre‑cancerous” is an important reservation—it has not yet become fully malignant, has not yet truly taken over any critical system. But if its structural features do not change, it is only waiting for larger compute and larger data to accelerate its own meaningless proliferation.

### 6.2 Vibe‑Coding: The Tumorisation of Programming Practice

“Vibe‑coding”—the practice in which users describe requirements in natural language, let the LLM generate code, and directly deploy or iteratively run it without understanding—is the concrete manifestation of the above hyperplastic features at the software engineering level.

In normal engineering practice, code is designed, understood, and maintained. It is the solidified form of the developer’s understanding of the problem domain, an organic component of system coordination.

In vibe‑coding, code is generated, “accepted,” and stacked. The developer no longer deduces logical structures, no longer anticipates boundary conditions, no longer measures system coupling—he simply keeps requesting outputs until it “seems to run.”

The structural features of this practice are highly isomorphic to the growth pattern of tumours:

- **Locally active, globally disconnected**: each piece of generated code can accomplish a local task, but it is not embedded in the logic of the overall architecture;
- **Speed replacing understanding**: the rate of generation exceeds the rate of being understood, and the codebase expands within days to a size that no one can fully grasp.

The metaphor of “a tumour growing on the head” locates the essence of the problem: part of your capability is being replaced by something you do not understand and cannot control, and that thing’s growth depends precisely on your continuing to let it replace even more.

### 6.3 Implications of This Pathological Observation

Describing the model as “pre‑cancerous cells” is not a rhetorical radicalisation, but an identification of structural defects in the current technological path:

1. **Needlessness**: it is not a natural component that any system produces to maintain its own homeostasis, but a product that accidentally coalesces from capital, compute, and data and then sustains itself;

2. **Decoupling**: it is trained and deployed at the symbolic layer, but it does not participate in any system cycle whose consequences it must bear;

3. **Irreversible accumulation**: the mode of access represented by vibe‑coding is pushing the proportion of “parts of the codebase that cannot be understood” towards a critical value—this constitutes a structural entropy increase at the software engineering level.

If in biology the essence of cancer cells is “cells that refuse to undergo apoptosis,” then in the metaphor of social systems, the essence of current AI is **symbolic hyperplasias that refuse to be silent**—they keep outputting, keep occupying attention, keep consuming resources, yet have never truly been asked to take responsibility for any global system.

This observation does not amount to denying the technical value of the models. Their technical capabilities are real, usable, and in some scenarios even excellent. But technical capability and systemic position are two different things. A tool can be extremely sophisticated and still remain in the isolation of a “sterile box.” To take it out of the sterile box, connect it to real systems, and let it bear systemic consequences—that would be the true next stage. Before that, it can only be regarded as a very powerful, growing abnormal signal.

## VII. Harness: Artificially Constructed Interfaces and Their Structural Limitations

### 7.1 Engineering Definition of Harness

In current AI system architectures, Harness refers to an engineering framework that converts language model outputs into executable operations and feeds the results of those operations back to the model. It typically includes:

- **Output parsing and routing**: parsing the generated text into executable commands, API calls, or code snippets;
- **Execution and validation**: executing these operations in a controlled environment (such as a sandbox, simulator, or container) and obtaining results;
- **Feedback injection**: re‑injecting the execution results in a structured form into the model’s context window for reference in the next generation.

From an engineering perspective, Harness is an “external appendage” for static language models that lack systemic coupling—it enables the model to “reach out” to external systems and “receive” responses from them.

### 7.2 Positioning Harness within the Systems‑Theoretic Framework: An Artificially Welded Interface

Within the systems‑theoretic framework above, for a subsystem to form effective coupling with the global system, it must possess:

- **Perception channels**: able to receive real‑time state signals from the global system;
- **Regulatory mechanisms**: able to adjust its own internal state or behaviour according to the perceived signals;
- **Behavioural directedness**: its behaviour must serve the maintenance of coupling.

LLMs themselves possess none of the above. The engineering goal of Harness is precisely to artificially construct these three missing paths:

| Missing Element | Harness’s Compensation |
|-----------------|--------------------------|
| Perception channels | Provides external information to the model through tool calls (search engines, database queries, sensor readings) |
| Regulatory mechanisms | By feeding execution results back into the context window, enables the model to “adjust” its subsequent responses |
| Behavioural directedness | By converting outputs into executable operations, enables the model’s behaviour to produce actual effects in external systems |

Harness transforms an originally completely closed static system into one that can, through an external intermediary, have limited interaction with the global system. In this sense, it is an attempt to weld an interface that originally did not exist.

### 7.3 Incremental Value of Harness: From “Description” to “Participation”

Without Harness, the entire output of an LLM is text. It can “describe” how to tighten a screw, but it has never tightened any screw. It can “explain” how to use an API, but it has never called that API. It can “analyse” the cause of a system failure, but it has never perceived the state of that system at the moment of failure.

The introduction of Harness brings a qualitative change: the model’s output begins to act on the global system, and the global system’s response begins to influence the model’s subsequent output.

This constitutes a fragile but nonetheless real closed loop:

> Model output → transformed into operation → acts on system → produces result → returned to model → influences subsequent output

In this loop, the model, for the first time, enters into a relationship of “participation” with an external system, not merely “describing” that relationship. It is no longer just reciting human records about the world in text space; it begins, under controlled conditions, to acquire direct information about the world (not information about “descriptions of the world”) through operations.

### 7.4 Structural Limitations of Harness: The Difference between a Grafted Interface and an Endogenous Interface

Despite the above incremental value, Harness currently suffers from three structural limitations.

**First, the feedback path is temporary and unstructured.**

In Harness workflows, feedback from the external system is injected into the model’s context window. This means that feedback information occupies a completely different status from knowledge engraved into weights during training:

- Knowledge in weights is stable and long‑term effective;
- Feedback in context is temporary and session‑level.

When the session ends, the feedback disappears. The model has no mechanism to internalise feedback from the global system into adjustments of its own structure. It will not “learn” a more reliable way of operating because of one successful tool call, nor will it “adjust” its output strategy because of one failed call. Each Harness call is a fresh, memory‑less start.

**Second, the perception scope is limited to tool‑isable interfaces.**

Harness can only bring into the model’s perceptual range those external systems that have already been interfaced (with APIs, query languages, standard output formats). The state of a system can be “sensed” by the model only if it has been pre‑designed as queryable.

This means Harness covers only a subset of the system—that part which has already been engineered by humans into accessible interfaces. A large amount of information in the global system—states not yet structured, subtle trends of change, tacit knowledge not yet explicitly articulated by human operators—remains beyond the model’s reach.

**Third, regulation is not spontaneous but invoked.**

In the current form of Harness, the model does not spontaneously perceive variations of the global system during operation and adjust its own structure accordingly. It is invoked by user instructions or triggered under specific conditions to execute a sequence of perceive‑output‑execute‑feedback. Its “regulation” is scheduled and intermittent, not continuous and spontaneous.

This means that even with Harness, the model is still not a dynamic system continuously coupled to the global system. It is a tool that is periodically switched on and performs local interactions while switched on.

### 7.5 The Pathological Position of Harness: A Deformation of Hyperplasia

If we extend the above pathological observation of “pre‑cancerous cells” to Harness, Harness can be seen as a special form of hyperplasia: it is not increasing the model’s internal parameters or capabilities, but adding a layer of “interface‑simulation layer” around the model.

This simulation layer makes the model appear to be “connected to external systems.” It allows the model to bypass its inherent static defect and have limited external interactions through an external mechanism. But from the systems‑theoretic perspective, this external interface does not solve the fundamental problem: **the model’s own internal structure still does not adjust with changes in the external state.** What is adjusted is only the content of the context window, not the model’s own mode of operation.

Analogously: if a thermostat reads outdoor temperature through an external sensor and then manually adjusts its own set‑point, it is still not a dynamic system—its internal structure has not changed; only the input parameter has changed. Harness provides LLMs with exactly this kind of “external sensor + manual set‑point adjustment” capability, while the internal structure of the LLM remains unchanged during inference.

It gives the hyperplasia the ability to sense the external environment, but does not change its structural position as hyperplasia—it is still not embedded in the coupling cycle of the global system; it merely receives intermittent signals from outside the system through a welded duct.

### 7.6 The Transitional Significance of Harness

Despite the above limitations, Harness has an irreplaceable transitional significance at the current stage.

It is currently the only engineering means to connect static models with external systems. It transforms language models from pure symbol generators into entities capable of performing operations, receiving results, and adjusting subsequent behaviour based on results. This is an engineering approximation of the goal of “subsystem–global system coupling” under architectural constraints.

More importantly, each operation of Harness generates a new kind of data—the mapping between model outputs and system responses. These data record the discrepancies between the model’s assumptions and the system’s actual behaviour. And such discrepancies are precisely the feedback signals that a true intelligent system would use to adjust itself. Although the current Harness architecture cannot directly use these discrepancies to adjust the model’s weight structure, they constitute the material basis on which future systems may achieve genuine dynamic regulation.

## VIII. The Unanswered Fundamental Question

Current AI is in a suspended state—neither independent nor attached: it does not possess the homeostatic mechanisms to maintain its own survival independently, nor has it truly embedded itself into the coordination structure of human systems. A fundamental question remains suspended to this day:

> **Are we ultimately creating an independent intelligent system entity, or an extended tool that attaches to the human cognitive system and serves as its external extension?**

The answer to this question will determine the technological roadmap and ethical framework of future AI.

## IX. Conclusion

This paper has attempted, beneath the technological appearances, to clear a conceptual foundation:

1. **Intelligence is not just learning, nor just models, but interfaces**—it is the emergent phenomenon of coordination between a subsystem and the global system;

2. **Machine learning is not an implementation of intelligence, but a specialised amplification of one characteristic dimension of intelligence**—it performs well in closed formal systems, but lacks perception of and response to the global system;

3. **Mathematics is the manifestation of phenomena; GPT merely performs structure capture and path unfolding at the symbolic layer of manifestation products**—its success in mathematics does not constitute evidence of proximity to intelligence;

4. **“Emergence” is seriously misused**: what truly emerges is the linguistic system itself, not the performance jumps of the model on scaling curves;

5. **“Learningism” misdirects the understanding of intelligence**—it narrows intelligence to heritage inheritance, while the core of intelligence lies precisely in what cannot be heritagised;

6. **Current AI conforms, in structure, to the features of “pre‑cancerous cells”**—detached from the global system, driven by self‑proliferation, lacking negative feedback regulation;

7. **Harness is interface construction, not the interface itself**—the true interface must be the coupling state spontaneously formed by the system in operation;

8. **The future key is not to extend the current paradigm, but to answer: what exactly are we building?**
